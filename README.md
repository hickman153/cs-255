## Project Summary

DriverPass was a client seeking a system to help students prepare for their driving tests. The proposed system needed to support online practice exams and learning materials as well as the scheduling and management of behind-the-wheel driving lessons.

The system also needed to support several types of users, including customers, instructors, office staff, administrators, and IT personnel. My role was to analyze the client's business needs and translate them into requirements and system design documentation.

## What I Did Well

One of the strongest aspects of my work was maintaining consistency between the client's stated needs, the system requirements, and the resulting design. I identified the major business functions and user roles and then carried those requirements through the process, object, and behavioral models.

This helped ensure that the design was based on the way DriverPass needed to operate rather than simply describing software features in isolation.

## Areas for Improvement

If I were to revise part of this work, I would make some of the nonfunctional requirements more specific and measurable. For example, I described the system as needing responsive performance without defining an acceptable response time. I also specified that an account should be locked after a defined number of unsuccessful login attempts without identifying that number.

I would revise requirements like these to include measurable targets so that developers have clear implementation goals and testers can objectively determine whether the requirements have been satisfied.

## Interpreting User Needs

I began with the needs expressed by the DriverPass stakeholders and translated them into specific system requirements. For example:

- Customers needed to schedule driving lessons.
- Office staff needed to schedule lessons on behalf of customers.
- Instructors and vehicles needed to be available without scheduling conflicts.
- Administrators needed appropriate control over the system.
- Training materials needed to remain current with DMV updates.

These needs influenced the system's functional requirements, user roles, data objects, and interactions.

Considering user needs is important because a technically functional system can still fail if it does not support the actual work its users need to perform. Requirements provide the connection between the business problem and the technical design. Keeping those requirements visible throughout the design process helps prevent the system from drifting away from its original purpose.

## My Approach to Software Design

I begin by identifying the stakeholders, understanding the business problem, and gathering both functional and nonfunctional requirements. From there, I use multiple types of models because each provides a different view of the system:

- **Process models** help explain workflows and the movement of information.
- **Object models** identify the major entities and their relationships.
- **Behavioral models** show how users and system components interact over time.

I would also continue validating the design against the original requirements throughout the process rather than treating requirements analysis as something that ends once design begins. Requirements can change as stakeholders better understand the proposed system, so continued communication, design reviews, and refinement are important.

This approach provides traceability from the user's needs to the final design and reduces the risk of building a system that works as designed but does not solve the intended problem.
